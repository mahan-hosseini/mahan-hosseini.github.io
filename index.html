Research Interests

Towards a quantitative, cross-species neuroscience of motor control.

I study how the nervous system controls movement, and how this control changes with ageing and disease across species. To make such comparisons possible at scale, I build open-source tools that automate the analysis of animal and human movement. With AutoGaitA, we showed that ageing affects fly, mouse and human gait in similar ways despite their distinct biomechanics. The next tool, AutoStepA, is still in development and will use machine learning models to split continuous movement recordings into behavioural episodes such as single steps.

I also investigate cross-species motor control of upper-limb movements. I currently am obtaining a multimodal (EEG and finger kinematics) dataset while healthy human subjects correct reaching movements in real time. This data will be used to identify the neural signatures of online motor control, how they break down in movement disorders and whether they differ from the control structures of monkeys.

During my PhD, I built the 2f-ST2 computational model accounting for distractor intrusion errors in rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) experiments. I also developed Dynamic Time Warping methods to measure the timing of EEG components, and showed how machine-learning classification of brain data can overfit in unexpected ways.

Education & Background

I am a Postdoctoral Researcher in Computational Neurology at the Institute of Neuroscience and Medicine (INM-3), Forschungszentrum Jülich, where I have worked with Prof Silvia Daun since 2023. I received my PhD in Computer Science from the University of Kent (2017–2022), supervised by Prof Howard Bowman and Dr Srivas Chennu, with a dissertation titled “My mind’s playing tricks on me. Understanding event integration in rapid stimulus streams.” Before that, I completed an MSc in Cognitive Neuroscience at Maastricht University (2015–2016, cum laude) and a BSc in Psychology at the University of Groningen (2012–2015).
